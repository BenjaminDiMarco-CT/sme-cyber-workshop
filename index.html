Meet our SME

A fictional WA homewares retailer. Work through the proposal form as if you were helping this business apply for cyber cover.

Revenue
AUD 18M. About 60% online through a hosted e-commerce platform, plus two Perth stores.
People & IT
45 staff and a small finance team. IT is outsourced to a local managed service provider (MSP).
Peak trading
November to December, from Black Friday to Christmas.
Data
Loyalty program with about 40,000 customers: names, emails, addresses and dates of birth. Card payments go through a third-party gateway, so no card data is stored.
Payments
About 150 suppliers paid by EFT, including overseas suppliers. Bank detail changes are accepted by email.
Tech & AI
Microsoft 365. Generative AI writes product copy, and the website has a chatbot.
Controls today
MFA on email but not on the e-commerce admin. Nightly cloud backups, but a restore has never been tested. Antivirus, no EDR. No incident response plan.

  2. What will the underwriter ask, and what's missing?

What an underwriter would flag for our retailerOpen this after the group discussion.
  • Gap
    MFA (F3, F4). MFA covers email but not the e-commerce admin, so the honest answer is "No" with a plan and timeframe. Ticking "Yes" here is a misstatement that can put the claim at risk later.
  • Gap
    Payments (I2, I4). Bank detail changes accepted by email is the classic social engineering setup. Expect social engineering cover to be declined or tightly sub-limited until call-backs on a known number are in place.
  • Gap
    Endpoint (E1). Antivirus only. Many insurers now expect EDR, often run by the MSP.
  • Gap
    Backups (D3, D4). Backups run nightly, but nobody has tested a restore and nobody knows how they're protected. Untested backups are a common reason a ransomware event turns into weeks of downtime.
  • Gap
    Plans (H1, H2). There's no incident response plan. For a business this size the insurer's incident response panel effectively becomes the plan, so the team needs to know the hotline and the first steps.
  • Ask
    Cover (B2 to B9). Size the limit to a realistic worst case: two weeks offline in peak season, plus responding to and notifying 40,000 loyalty members, plus liability claims. At this size a higher deductible rarely buys a meaningful saving, so keep it low and ask for a BI waiting period in hours, not days. Check the social engineering sub-limit against the largest supplier payment, and include dependent BI for the e-commerce platform and payment gateway.
  • Ask
    Data (D1). About 40,000 people's records. Dates of birth make the data more useful to identity thieves. Does the loyalty program actually need them?
  • Ask
    Dependencies (C1). The hosted e-commerce platform and payment gateway are single points of failure in peak season. Check contingent business interruption and the waiting period.
  • Ask
    AI and content. AI-written product copy and a chatbot raise media liability and chatbot data questions. Few short forms ask about this, but a broker should.
  • Good
    Cards (D5, D6). No card data is stored because a third-party gateway handles payments. Confirm the PCI DSS responsibilities in the gateway contract.
